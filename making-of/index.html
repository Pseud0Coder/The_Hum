Case study · The Hum × DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash

One prompt. 4,869 lines. A horror game that listens.

A single sentence asked for a browser survival-horror game "beyond imagination." What followed was a complete development cycle (architecture, implementation, playtesting, debugging, and public deployment) inside one agentic session. This is how it happened, and how the model's architecture shows up in the result.

01

The prompt

"build me a 3d survival horror game that runs on browser. I dont need a generic game, I need something that is beyond imagination"

the entire design brief

No design document. No reference art. No engine. The interpretation of "beyond imagination" was left to the model, and the choice it made is the game's entire identity: the monster hunts through your real microphone. Your keyboard, your chair, your breathing: noise in the room you are physically sitting in becomes the entity's hunting signal. Everything else follows from that one decision.

02

What was actually built

threat model

Your room is the enemy

Live microphone analysis calibrates the ambient floor of your room, then treats sound above it as a hunting signal. Real noise creates in-world noise events with distance-scaled radius. A "deaf" fallback exists if you decline the mic.

ai

Corridor-aware hearing

Sound propagates along the map graph, with walls adding a capped penalty, so doorways carry noise and sealed rooms muffle it. Sprint paths are extrapolated and intercepted. Hiding spots are remembered across sessions, and lockers you used before get checked first.

continuity

Your past runs walk again

Death routes are recorded and replayed as a translucent ghost of your previous run. Reach the end of the route and it materialises, solid, and now it knows your habits.

rendering

PS1 pipeline on WebGL

Low-resolution render target, vertex snapping, affine texture warping, Bayer dithering, chromatic aberration and film grain, applied through a custom post chain and shader injection into every lit material.

audio

18 synthesized sound generators

Drones, room hums, footsteps, heartbeats, whispers, screeches and the entity's speech cues, all built live on the Web Audio API. No audio files exist in the repository.

world

Procedural facility, 10 texture generators

Concrete, tile, metal, rust, hazard stripes, signs, posters, blood decals and more, drawn pixel by pixel on canvas at load. Core rooms re-randomise every run; the layout stays fixed so the entity's memory stays meaningful.

03

The development process

Phase 01

System before spectacle

Before a single corridor existed, the build was decomposed into twelve single-responsibility modules (world generation, entity AI, player controller, audio engine, post-processing, UI, persistence, taunts), with a vendored Three.js and no build step. That decision is why the game can be a GitHub Pages folder: plain ES modules, static hosting, nothing to compile.

Phase 02

Build with the loop closed

The agent did not write code blind. It started a local dev server, opened the game in a browser it could drive, evaluated JavaScript inside the running page, forced scenarios, read shader diagnostics and captured framebuffers. Verification happened continuously during construction, not as a phase at the end.

Phase 03

Playtest notes as specification

Once it was playable, the human became the director. Feedback arrived as plain sentences ("the entity can't see me at the door," "it's too easy," "make the text readable," "randomize the cores"), and each became a diagnosed root cause, a tested fix, and a new feature in the same pass. The six-commit history reads as feature bundles, not micro-churn.

Phase 04

Ship, then keep iterating

Deployed to GitHub Pages with automatic rebuilds on push. Post-release fixes were verified against the live URL, including a rebuild of the entity's senses, a panic mechanic tied to carrying cores, stimpacks, and a full typographic overhaul.

04

The debug log is the real benchmark

Benchmarks measure isolated tasks. A game accumulates coupled ones. These are real incidents from the build: the reported symptom, the actual root cause, and the fix. The pattern worth noticing: none were solved by guessing. Each required reading the system.

The world rendered black

reportedEverything is invisible except unlit signs and lamp glows.
root causeShader injection used uniforms that were never declared in the vertex stage; every lit material failed to compile. A second, deeper pass found the real culprit: vMapUv /= …, because WebGL2 forbids assigning to fragment varyings.
fixDeclared the missing uniforms, then replaced the write with a custom map_fragment sampling override that divides at read time.

Every refresh showed a black screen

reportedA fresh page load is permanently black; only navigation into a run clears it.
root causeThe full-screen fade element defaulted to opacity: 1 at z-index 50 (above every menu), and nothing cleared it on load.
fixDefault to transparent and explicitly clear it at boot.

Movement didn't match the camera

reported"The mouse control and WASD movement are not in sync."
root causeThe camera-relative basis had sign-flipped cross terms, making movement world-relative.
fixCorrected the rotation matrix, then verified all four quadrants numerically in the live page.

The E key did nothing

reported"Pressed E at the elevator with all four cores and nothing happens."
root causeThe player controller cleared the interact flag at the end of its own update, before the interaction logic read it. Hiding was silently broken too.
fixReplaced the clear-on-update with an explicit consumeInteract(); verified both the elevator and locker paths.

A constant drone under everything

reported"What is that constant monotonous droning noise still coming in the background?"
root causeA tremolo oscillator was wired additively into the tension gain at ±0.5 amplitude, so two high tones played at half volume permanently, regardless of tension.
fixScaled the modulator 166× down and lowered the tension level; the bed only responds to real tension now.

The entity couldn't see or reach the player

reported"It has trouble seeing me or passing through the door even though I'm standing right next to it."
root causeThe line-of-sight ray treated every prop as a wall, and if the entity ever stopped inside a prop collider, it was permanently blind. Paths could also grind against a doorway forever.
fixOnly walls and doors block sight, a proximity sense triggers under 2.3 m, and a stuck-escape watchdog re-paths after 1.2 s without displacement.

Ghosts killed you at spawn

reportedRuns ended within seconds of starting, repeatedly.
root causeA previous run's recorded route starts at the spawn point. The replaying ghost inherited full detection and attacked immediately.
fixGhost replay is non-lethal and detection-disabled until it materialises, plus a spawn grace period.

The mouse vanished on death

reported"The mouse pointer does not show up when you die."
root causePointer lock was never released, so the death screen had no usable cursor.
fixRelease pointer lock the moment the death state begins.
05

Architecture, cross-referenced

What the model is, per its public model card. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is a multimodal mixture-of-experts model (552B backbone parameters, up to a 1M-token context) built around a Causal Encoder-Decoder that projects the decoder's KV cache from the encoder's final hidden states. That design activates only 8B parameters per token during prefill and 16B during decode, which is precisely the profile of agentic coding: enormously input-heavy, comparatively output-light. Its Compressed Sparse Attention 2 and FP4 KV caching shrink the global KV footprint to 890 bytes per token, roughly ¼ of its predecessor and 1/437th of the first generation. Add Engram conditional memory, DSpark speculative decoding, and a controllable reasoning effort from 1 to 100, and you have a model explicitly tuned for long-horizon work in a real environment.

The decoder's KV is projected from the encoder's final hidden states instead of every decoder layer's own, decoupling input cost from output cost.

documented architecture

Agentic coding is exactly the workload this optimises: thousands of lines of context and tool output going in, comparatively little coming out. Cheap prefill meant verification could run on every change instead of at milestones.

Images and text are processed jointly from the start of pretraining through a vision encoder and projector.

documented architecture

A game is a visual artefact, and visual self-inspection closes the loop that text-only agents cannot: seeing the actual output, then judging lighting, readability and composition the way a player would.

Controllable reasoning effort · 1 to 100

Inference cost can be traded against accuracy continuously per task.

documented architecture

The hard problems in this build were never syntactic. They were semantic: why a fragment shader silently fails, why a modulator creates a permanent tone, why an input flag disappears between two lines. Reasoning spent on system semantics is what found those, not pattern-matching.

Semi-autoregressive drafting with confidence-scheduled verification accelerates generation, especially for long, structured output.

documented architecture

Large files land complete and coherent in one pass, which keeps the iteration cadence limited by testing speed rather than writing speed. A 1,000-line module arriving in one shot is a different development rhythm than assembling it piecewise.

06

What the architecture didn't do

The model could not:

  • Hear the mix. The droning bug was found by a human ear. The agent could inspect the audio graph but not experience the result.
  • Feel difficulty. Two clean escapes without dying were the human's observation, and that judgement drove the entire AI-pressure redesign.
  • Decide what the game is called. The human renamed it to THE HUM and turned the title into the entity's name and the lore.
  • Judge taste. "Beyond imagination" was an instruction the model interpreted, but the standard it was held to was human.

What that means in practice

  • The bottleneck moved. From writing code to describing intent precisely. The most valuable human input was a one-line symptom or a taste decision, not an implementation.
  • Direction stayed human. Every root-cause fix and feature was initiated by an observation only a player could make.
  • Architecture compounds. Cheap long-context inference doesn't just save money; it changes the method. Continuous verification, whole-project edits and same-session features are only rational when context and iteration are cheap.

The honest summary: the architecture made a long, coupled, real project tractable in a single session. The human made it good.
